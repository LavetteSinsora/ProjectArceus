Why a curiosity-driven agent suddenly stops exploring — and two smaller surprises along the way

An exploration agent on a sparse-reward puzzle game shows three unexpected behaviors. They have three separate causes. The headline one — a sudden, one-time collapse of exploration — is a clean dynamical-systems effect, not the bug it first looks like.

The setup, in plain terms. An agent explores a puzzle game (LS20) where a reward comes only on solving the level, so it must explore on its own. Two parts: a policy (a network that picks one of 4 actions, trained by REINFORCE — the most basic policy-gradient method, here with no value-function "baseline"), and a curiosity bonus that rewards reaching unfamiliar states (Random Network Distillation: a fixed random "target" net and a learned "predictor" net; the predictor's leftover error — large for new states, ~0 for familiar — is the "novelty" reward). Novelty is computed on a feature vector from a small CNN encoder that is trained by inverse dynamics (predict which action happened between two frames). Success = environment steps to the first reward.

The three findings in one line each.
A. The encoder learns useful (action-predictive) features only because of the timer mask.
B. The novelty numbers balloon because the encoder's output is never normalized, so its magnitude grows and novelty ∝ magnitude².
C (the headline). The policy suddenly freezes on the small game because it runs out of new states: the curiosity reward loses its information, and a plain REINFORCE policy then diffuses into a single action. A control experiment shows this is not caused by B — normalizing the features shrinks the novelty numbers but the policy still freezes, so running out of new states is the real cause — the growing feature magnitude only changed the size of the numbers.

Part A — Two quick wins (the settled causes)

A1 · The encoder learns action-predictive features — because of the timer mask

Inverse-dynamics accuracy reaches ~1.0, which earlier versions of this project never achieved. The single cause is blanking the on-screen step-timer: otherwise the timer advances every step, every frame looks unique for no real reason, and accuracy sits at chance (0.25). It is not the feature magnitude — forcing features to unit length leaves accuracy at 1.0. (Honest accuracy over all steps is ~0.62, because "wall-bump" steps don't change the board so their action is genuinely unrecoverable.)

A2 · The novelty numbers balloon — because the encoder output isn't normalized

The inverse-dynamics objective is the only thing that trains this encoder. To predict actions more sharply it simply grows the magnitude of its output vector, stopping once accuracy saturates. Since novelty is measured on these un-normalized features, the novelty score scales with magnitude² (correlation with the predictor's error = 0.9998). So "the error rises instead of falling" is a ruler-stretching side-effect, not a learning failure.

Part B — The headline: why exploration suddenly collapses

B1 · The puzzle

Same algorithm, opposite outcomes. On the small game (LS20-L1) the policy is fine for ~90k steps, then entropy crashes almost vertically to 0 and the agent just bumps a wall forever. On the large game (TU93-L3) it never collapses. And the crash is one-time: there's an earlier, equally-deep wobble that fully recovers. The one quantity that tracks the difference is the sign of the curiosity reward — negative on the small game, positive on the large one.

B2 · Why the obvious explanation is wrong

The tempting story is "the reward goes negative, so it pushes action probabilities down and the policy collapses." That's not how it works. A reward that is the same for every action produces, on average, zero net change to every action's probability: the push-down an action gets when it's chosen is exactly cancelled by the push-up it gets (relative to others) when a different action is chosen. And a nearly-deterministic policy receives almost no gradient at all (the policy-gradient term shrinks to zero as one action's probability approaches 1). So a constant negative reward neither pushes toward nor away from any action.

Why "the sharpest action gets pushed down the most, so it self-corrects" fails: that action is also pushed up every time another action is sampled, and the two cancel exactly — so there is no self-correction, in either direction.

B3 · The real mechanism — a driftless random walk

Zero average change does not mean nothing happens: it means the policy's internal scores do a random walk (the updates have zero mean but nonzero variance). Entropy is highest at the perfectly-uniform point and falls as the scores drift apart — and with no force pulling them back to uniform, the random walk inevitably spreads them out, so entropy wanders down to 0. A toy REINFORCE confirms it: a constant reward of either sign collapses entropy, and it collapses onto a random action (different each run) — the signature of diffusion, not a directed push. Only an informative reward (different per action) collapses onto a specific action.

B4 · What actually held exploration up — and why it vanished

So what kept the policy exploring during the good early phase, if a positive constant reward would also diffuse? The answer: early on the reward is informative, not constant — different states genuinely have different novelty (high reward spread), and that spread is a real, directional signal that keeps steering the agent toward new states. That informativeness is the restoring force. When the small game is fully explored, every state becomes equally stale, the spread collapses (it falls from ~1.4 to ~0.01), and the reward becomes effectively constant — so only the driftless random walk is left, and the policy diffuses into a corner. The large game never runs out of genuinely-new states, so its reward stays informative and the restoring force never disappears.

B5 · Why it's sudden and one-time

The exploring policy is metastable, and its stability flips the moment the reward loses its information (~update 41). Before the flip, the informative reward restores small wobbles — which is exactly why an earlier near-collapse around update 30 fully recovers. After the flip there is no restoring force, so the next wobble — the same size as the one that recovered — runs away instead. It looks instantaneous because the action probabilities are a softmax (exponential): once one action edges ahead, it saturates toward probability 1 within a couple of updates, and the narrowing policy stops visiting varied states, removing the last bit of corrective signal. The exact timing is random (whichever wobble first escapes after the flip), which is why it's a one-time crash, not a slow slide.

B6 · Two controls pin the cause

(i) It is not the feature magnitude (Part A2). Normalizing the encoder output shrinks the novelty numbers ~140× — yet the policy still freezes. So the ballooning magnitude only set the size of the numbers, not the reason for the collapse. Result: the policy still freezes even with normalized features (final entropy 0.00; curiosity reward negative on 97% of updates) — so running out of new states is the real cause. Normalizing only shrank the novelty score about 142× (from ~314 to ~2.2), confirming the feature magnitude was just the size of the numbers, not the reason the policy stops exploring.

(ii) A value baseline would prevent this specific collapse. A baseline subtracts the running average return, which removes the constant component — and with it the random walk. The policy would then freeze its current entropy rather than crash to a single action once the reward goes uninformative. So the "no baseline" choice is genuinely load-bearing for the collapse (not just a minor aggravator) — though it doesn't restore the missing exploration signal.

The bottom line

Three independent causes.  (A) the timer mask makes the features action-predictive;  (B) not normalizing the encoder output makes the novelty numbers balloon;  (C) on a small game the curiosity reward eventually runs out of information, and a baseline-free REINFORCE policy then diffuses into one action (a driftless random walk, not a push) — sharply, because softmax saturates.

Fixes. Keep the timer mask. Normalize/whiten the encoder output to stop the novelty numbers ballooning. For the collapse, two complementary cures: a value baseline (removes the random walk → the policy freezes gracefully instead of crashing), and a curiosity signal that doesn't fade once everything is seen or a switch to exploiting the real reward once the game is explored (restores the missing direction). Note the collapse happens after success anyway — the first reward came at ~33,000 steps, faster than a random policy (~50,000).
